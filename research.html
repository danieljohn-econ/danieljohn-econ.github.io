Job Market Paper

The Cost of Credibility: Wage Catch-Up and Borrower Protection after Supply Shocks

PDF

I study how wage adjustment after supply shocks affects household income, spending and debt. Using US data for 1965–2019, a sign-restricted Bayesian VAR and local projections, I find that adverse supply shocks raise nominal wages in tight labour markets before 1990 but lower them after 1990. This reversal is accompanied by early declines in nominal labour income, larger spending cuts and deeper debt contractions. Expansionary demand shocks instead raise wages by more after 1990, weighing against a general weakening of wage responsiveness. Greater monetary credibility offers a plausible explanation, consistent with external evidence of weaker wage catch-up. Under this interpretation, weaker nominal-income protection and less early inflation erosion of debt leave borrowers more exposed, and the larger spending losses are a recurring cost of credibility that the sacrifice ratio does not measure.

Methods: Sign-restricted Bayesian VAR · State-dependent local projections

Working Paper

Wages and Common Inflation Dynamics: Evidence from US Spending Categories (coming soon)

I examine whether lower estimated pass-through from wages to prices means that wage growth has become less inflationary. Using US spending-category data since 1960, I construct a model in which goods prices, wages and services prices respond to common pressures at different speeds and wages never affect prices. Standard VAR procedures applied to data simulated from this model nevertheless show the decline in labour-cost pass-through found in the actual data, from about one half before 1985 to almost zero after 1990. This apparent wage effect arises because wages carry information about the common pressures that also move prices. The findings caution against treating weak estimated wage-to-price pass-through as sufficient evidence that inflation pressures will subside or that monetary policy can support employment with little inflation risk.

Methods: Simulated common-factor model · Bayesian VAR
